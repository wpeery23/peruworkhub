EOR Comparison: How to Hire in Peru

Last verified: 2026-10-17. Every pricing figure below is copied from the cited source. Where no figure is shown, pricing was not confirmed in the evidence reviewed — not omitted by accident.

This page is a neutral comparison. It is general information, not legal advice. Peruvian labor rules are jurisdiction-specific: consult a Peruvian labor lawyer before relying on any employment-structure decision described here.

How hiring in Peru works

A foreign company that wants someone working from Peru generally chooses one of three routes. The right route depends on headcount, duration, and risk tolerance — this page describes the routes in general terms; it does not recommend one for your situation.

1. Employer of Record (EOR)

An EOR company becomes the legal employer of the worker in Peru on the client's behalf. The EOR typically handles the local employment contract, payroll, tax withholding, and statutory benefits, while the client directs the worker's day-to-day work. Providers state they handle Peru-specific items such as gratificaciones (13th/14th-month bonuses), CTS (severance savings), EsSalud (public health insurance), and PLAME payroll reporting. Verify before relying on it: the exact list of statutory obligations, contribution rates, and filing requirements for a specific hire should be confirmed with the provider and a Peruvian labor lawyer, because provider marketing pages summarize rather than reproduce the law.

2. Contractor

The worker is engaged as an independent contractor rather than an employee. This is usually simpler and cheaper, but it carries misclassification risk: if the working relationship looks like employment in practice (set hours, direct supervision, exclusivity), authorities may treat it as employment regardless of the contract label. Verify before relying on it: the specific misclassification triggers and penalties in Peru are jurisdiction-specific and are not stated as fact on this page.

3. Own local entity

The company sets up its own Peruvian legal entity and employs workers directly. This gives the most control but requires entity setup, local registration, and ongoing compliance administration. One provider (Payoneer Workforce Management) states entity setup takes roughly 4–6 weeks; that is that provider's figure, cited below, not a verified general timeline. Verify before relying on it: entity-setup timelines and costs vary by entity type and circumstances.

What the fee numbers below do and do not include: EOR service fees are generally charged on top of the employee's salary plus employer statutory contributions and benefits. A low service fee does not mean a low total cost. Always ask each provider for a full quote: service fee + employer contributions + benefits + any deposits, FX charges, or termination fees.

How to use this comparison

  • Confirm the live price with the provider — published figures change, and several providers above do not publish Peru-specific pricing at all.
  • Ask for the full cost: service fee + employer statutory contributions + benefits + deposits + FX/conversion charges + termination terms.
  • Ask who the employing legal entity is in Peru, and whether the provider uses its own entity or a local partner.
  • Ask for onboarding timeline, contract language (Spanish-language contracts matter in Peru), and how terminations are handled.
  • Do not treat this page as legal advice. For a specific hire, consult a Peruvian labor lawyer.
